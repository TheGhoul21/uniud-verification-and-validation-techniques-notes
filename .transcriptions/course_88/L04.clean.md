Today we wrap up our review of automata theory. In the first two lectures, we introduced three classes of finite-state automata: deterministic finite automata (DFA), non-deterministic finite automata (NFA), and NFAs with epsilon moves. We showed that these three classes are equally expressive. Because of this equivalence, we can choose the most suitable model depending on our goal.

We then moved to regular expressions. We introduced general regular expressions, restricted regular expressions, and star-free regular expressions. We showed that general and restricted regular expressions are equivalent to each other and are as expressive as finite-state automata. We left open the question of whether star-free regular expressions are as expressive as the other classes; we will return to this topic in the second semester.

We also proved that regular languages are closed under union, intersection, complementation, concatenation, and Kleene star. Closure properties are crucial because they allow us to decide other language problems, such as the inclusion (model checking) problem, the universality problem, and the equivalence problem, by reducing them to the emptiness problem. This reduction relies on closure under intersection and complementation.

Checking for emptiness (i.e., whether the language is empty) may seem trivial or of little practical use. However, if we think of automata as a way to represent logical formulas, the emptiness problem corresponds to satisfiability checking in logic. In verification, where formulas specify the expected behavior of a complex system, satisfiability checking acts as a sanity check to ensure the specification is consistent and does not contain contradictions. We solve satisfiability by translating formulas into automata and checking them for emptiness.

We show that the emptiness problem for regular languages is decidable using a small model theorem. The techniques used in this proof are simple but general, and we will apply similar methods to more complex problems in the second semester.

We state the following theorem: the emptiness problem, checking whether the language of a given automaton is empty, is decidable.

If we can prove this theorem, the decidability of the emptiness problem follows. To check whether the language is empty, we can simply execute the automaton on all words of length less than n. Since the alphabet is finite, the number of such words is finite, so this procedure terminates. If the automaton accepts at least one of these words, the language is non-empty; if it rejects all of them, the language is empty. While there are more efficient procedures, this simple approach is sufficient to establish decidability.

One direction of the theorem is trivial: if there exists a word in the language of length less than n, then the language is obviously non-empty. The interesting direction is the converse: if the language is non-empty, there must exist a word in the language of length less than n.

We prove this by contradiction. Assume that the language is non-empty, but all words in the language have length greater than or equal to n. Since the language is non-empty, we can choose a word z of minimal length. By assumption, the length of z, which we denote by m, must satisfy m >= n. Let z equal the sequence of symbols a1 through am.

Since z is in the language, there exists a successful computation of the automaton on z. Let this computation be p0 through pm, where p0 is the initial state, and pm is a final state. This computation involves m+1 states. Since m >= n, the number of states visited in this computation, m+1, is strictly greater than the total number of states in the automaton, n. By the pigeonhole principle, at least one state must be visited more than once.

Let pi be the state reached from the initial state after reading the prefix a1 through ai of z for each i. Thus, p0 is the initial state, and pm is the final state reached at the end of the computation. Since the computation visits m+1 states and the automaton has only n distinct states, with m >= n, there must be a state that is visited at least twice. This means there exist indices i, j with i < j such that pi = pj.

We can split the word z into three parts: u, v, and w, where u equals a1 through ai, v equals a-i-plus-1 through aj, and w equals a-j-plus-1 through am. Since i < j, the segment v contains at least one symbol. This decomposition corresponds to a path in the automaton: we start in the initial state, reach state pi by reading u, loop from pi back to itself by reading v, and finally reach the final state pm from pi by reading w.

Since the automaton is deterministic, we can construct a valid computation for the word uw by skipping the loop v. This computation starts in the initial state, reaches pi by reading u, and then proceeds to the final state pm by reading w. Therefore, the word uw belongs to the language.

This construction is closely related to the pumping lemma, which states that if uvw is accepted, then u v to the k w is accepted for all k >= 0. By taking k = 0, we get the word uw.

Because v is non-empty, the length of uw is strictly less than the length of z. This contradicts our hypothesis that z is a word in the language of minimal length. Therefore, our assumption that all words in the language have length at least n must be false. This completes the proof of the small model theorem.

To check whether the language recognized by an automaton is empty, we do not need to perform an exhaustive search of all words of length less than n. A much simpler procedure is to treat the automaton as a directed graph. The emptiness problem then reduces to reachability: checking whether there exists a path in the graph from the initial state to some final state.

We can solve this reachability problem using a standard fixed-point computation. We start with the set containing only the initial state. At each step, we compute the set of all states reachable in one step from the current set. We repeat this until we either reach a final state (in which case the language is non-empty) or the set of reachable states does not grow any larger (in which case the language is empty). Since the number of states is finite, this algorithm is guaranteed to terminate.

We now introduce the Myhill-Nerode theorem. To prove it, we must define some basic concepts, starting with right-invariant equivalence relations.

Let the set of all finite words over an alphabet, which is closed under concatenation, be our domain. An equivalence relation tilde is right-invariant if for all words x and y, if x is equivalent to y, then for all words z, the concatenation xz is equivalent to yz. This means that if two words belong to the same equivalence class, appending the same suffix to both words will yield two new words that also belong to the same equivalence class.

We now introduce a specific equivalence relation associated with a language L. We denote this relation by tilde L. Two words x and y are tilde L related if and only if, for all words z, xz belongs to L if and only if yz belongs to L. This means that x and y behave identically with respect to right-concatenation and membership in L. If appending z to x yields a word in L, then appending z to y must also yield a word in L, and vice versa.

We can show that tilde L is an equivalence relation. Furthermore, tilde L is right-invariant. To prove this, let x and y be two tilde L related words, and let v be an arbitrary word. We must show that xv is also tilde L related to yv. By definition, this means we must show that for all z, xvz belongs to L if and only if yvz belongs to L.

We can view xvz as the word x concatenated with the suffix vz. Similarly, yvz is y concatenated with vz. Since x and y are tilde L related by hypothesis, they behave identically under concatenation with any suffix. Choosing the suffix vz, we conclude that x concatenated with vz belongs to L if and only if y concatenated with vz belongs to L. This is exactly the condition required to prove that xv and yv are tilde L related. Therefore, tilde L is right-invariant.

Next, we define an equivalence relation associated with a deterministic finite automaton A. We denote this relation by tilde A. Two words x and y are tilde A related if and only if, when starting from the initial state q0, they lead to the same state in the automaton.

The relation tilde A satisfies three properties:
1. It is an equivalence relation.
2. It is right-invariant.
3. It has a finite index.

The index of an equivalence relation is the number of its equivalence classes. For tilde A, the equivalence classes partition the set of finite words according to the state reached in the automaton. Since the number of states is finite, the number of classes cannot exceed the number of states. Therefore, the index of tilde A is finite and bounded by the cardinality of the state set.

We are now ready to state the Myhill-Nerode theorem, which is a classic result in automata theory. This theorem will be useful in several contexts, including automata learning (where one reconstructs the structure of an automaton through queries).

Theorem: Let L be a language. The following three statements are equivalent:
1. L is a regular language.
2. L can be expressed as a finite union of equivalence classes of a right-invariant equivalence relation of finite index.
3. The relation tilde L is of finite index.

To prove this theorem, we will show that (1) implies (2), (2) implies (3), and (3) implies (1), thereby closing the loop.

We first prove that (1) implies (2). Let L be a regular language. By definition, there exists a DFA A that recognizes L. The relation tilde A associated with this DFA is an equivalence relation, is right-invariant, and has a finite index. We only need to show that L can be defined as a finite union of classes of tilde A.

The equivalence classes of tilde A partition the set of finite words based on the state reached in the automaton. A word x belongs to L if and only if it leads the automaton from the initial state q0 to some final state in F. Therefore, L is exactly the union of all equivalence classes of tilde A that correspond to final states. Since the set of final states is finite, this union is finite. This proves that (1) implies (2).

Next, we prove that (2) implies (3). Let tilde be an equivalence relation that satisfies the conditions of statement (2): it is right-invariant, has a finite index, and L is a union of its equivalence classes. We must show that tilde L has a finite index. To do this, it suffices to show that tilde is a refinement of tilde L.

An equivalence relation tilde is a refinement of tilde L if for all words x and y, if x is equivalent to y under tilde, then they are also equivalent under tilde L. Geometrically, this means the partition induced by tilde is finer than the partition induced by tilde L: each equivalence class of tilde L is a union of one or more classes of tilde. If tilde is a refinement of tilde L, the number of classes of tilde L must be less than or equal to the number of classes of tilde. Since the index of tilde is finite, the index of tilde L must also be finite.

To prove that tilde is a refinement of tilde L, assume x is equivalent to y under tilde. Since tilde is right-invariant, for all words z, we have xz is equivalent to yz under tilde. Since L is a union of equivalence classes of tilde, any class of tilde is either completely contained in L or completely disjoint from it. Therefore, since xz and yz belong to the same class of tilde, xz belongs to L if and only if yz belongs to L. This is exactly the definition of x and y being equivalent under tilde L. Thus, tilde is a refinement of tilde L, and statement (3) holds.

Finally, we prove that (3) implies (1). Assume tilde L has a finite index. We must construct a DFA A prime that recognizes L. The set of states Q prime is chosen to be the quotient set of finite words modulo tilde L (the set of equivalence classes of tilde L). Since the index of tilde L is finite, Q prime is a finite set. Let [x] denote the equivalence class containing x.

We define the components of the DFA A prime as follows:
1. The transition function Delta prime is defined for any class [x] and symbol a as Delta prime([x], a) = [xa]. This definition is well-defined because tilde L is right-invariant.
2. The initial state q0 prime is the equivalence class containing the empty word: q0 prime = [epsilon].
3. The set of final states F prime contains all classes [x] such that x belongs to L. This is well-defined because if two words belong to the same class of tilde L, they must behave identically with respect to membership in L.

This constructive proof shows that any language with a finite index of tilde L is regular. Furthermore, since any right-invariant relation satisfying (2) is a refinement of tilde L, the automaton constructed from tilde L has the minimum possible number of states. This provides a characterization of the minimal automaton for a regular language.

We stop here.
